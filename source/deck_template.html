PROTEUS explained
PROTEUS · how it works, from A to Z

A contrast-enhanced ultrasound simulator in four modules

PROTEUS simulates what an ultrasound probe records when microbubbles flow through a vessel tree: realistic flow, a full-wave acoustic simulation (k-Wave), nonlinear bubble dynamics, and the image reconstruction. This deck follows one simulation from the flow data to the image. Figures and videos were produced by running the PROTEUS code itself (revamp branch).

1 · Flow → ground truth

streamline-module: microbubbles are tracked through a CFD velocity field. Output: bubble positions, velocities and radii for every frame and pulse.

2 · Acoustics

acoustic-module: builds the grid, tissue medium and transducer, runs k-Wave for the transmitted wave and propagates the bubble echoes back to the probe. Output: RF data per element.

3 · Microbubbles

microbubble-simulator: a Rayleigh-Plesset ODE with a lipid shell turns the pressure each bubble feels into the volume pulsation that radiates its echo.

4 · Reconstruction

delay-and-sum: RF data → B-mode images (reference beamformer and the general one for curved arrays).

Around them

GUIfunctions (settings and the 7 GUI panels), mesh-voxelisation (vessel STL → grid), geometry_data (flow geometries), Transducer-models (calibrations).

Navigate

Arrow keys or the buttons; Contents lists every slide. Each slide names the functions involved so you can open them next to it.

The big picture

Data flow through PROTEUS

Two inputs (a flow simulation and the settings from the GUI) feed the RF simulation; the RF data feed the image reconstruction. Every arrow is a file on disk, so each stage can be rerun on its own.

Before anything else

One coordinate system, and the settings structs

Imaging plane = xy (z = 0). Arrays and images are indexed in this order: a pressure map or image is Nx×Ny (depth × lateral). Points outside the grid raise an error.

Module 1 · streamline-module

From a CFD velocity field to bubble positions

What happens

  1. vtu2matlab converts the Musubi .vtu (points, cells, velocity) into vtu.mat plus the inlet and geometry properties.
  2. generate_streamlines draws a start position in the vessel (draw_start_position), and integrates dx/dt = v(x) with ode23 (get_velocity looks up the cell velocity).
  3. Trajectories are sampled at every acquisition time: frames at the frame rate, and pulses inside a frame at the time between pulses.
  4. Radii come from the size distribution (draw_random_radii); a bubble that leaves the vessel (exitVesselFcn) is replaced at the inlet, drawn from a density map so that all branches stay populated.

Output, per frame: Frame.PulseN.Points (N×3, geometry units), Velocity, Radius, StreamNumber. load_microbubbles later moves them into simulation coordinates: subtract the bounding-box centre, rotate (Geometry.Rotation), add Geometry.Center.

Module 1 · where the velocity comes from

How flow simulation data is used

From CFD to trajectories

  1. Get a flow field: the example data on Zenodo, or your own simulation (the lattice-Boltzmann solver Musubi, or any CFD exporting a .vtu). Usually a converged, steady-state result.
  2. Convert it: vtu2matlab writes vtu.mat and the geometry files. You set the units and the name of the velocity field in vtuProperties.
  3. Build the inlet: backpropagation runs tracers backwards with −v, filter_points keeps those ending on the inlet, get_probability_density turns them into the injection density.
  4. Load: load_vessel_data drops zero-velocity cells and builds a sparse lookup from Cartesian cell to velocity.
  5. Track: ode23 calls get_velocity: position → nearest cell → velocity. It stops when the cell is outside the vessel.
What PROTEUS reads: only the velocity field. Pressure or other fields in the VTU are not used. The field is a single snapshot, frozen in time, so pulsatile flow is not represented. Bubbles follow it as passive tracers (no drag, buoyancy or acoustic forces) and the velocity is piecewise constant per cell.
Module 2 · acoustic-module · setting up

The simulation domain: just big enough for the beam and the geometry

compute_simulation_domain takes the transducer surface and its projection to the deepest point of the geometry (beam shape from the elevation focus and the lateral focus or virtual source), adds a margin of 2 λ, and keeps the box. define_grid then sets the grid spacing dx = c/(f0·PPW) (default 6 points per wavelength) and the time step dt = CFL·dx/c (CFL 0.3), and chooses the PML thickness (≥ 15 points) so that every dimension has small prime factors, which keeps k-Wave's FFTs fast.

Setting up · the medium

Tissue, speckle, vessel, coupling region

define_medium builds the voxel maps k-Wave needs

  • Speckle: sound speed and density are multiplied by 1 + σ·n, with n a normal draw truncated at ±2σ (random_truncated_normal, σ = 2 %). These sub-wavelength fluctuations are the linear scatterers that make tissue echoes.
  • Vessel: the STL surface of the geometry is voxelised (VOXELISE, mesh-voxelisation) after the same rotation and translation as the bubbles; inside, the properties of blood.
  • Attenuation: power law α = α0·fy; BonA for nonlinearity.
  • Curved arrays: no speckle inside the circle of curvature (the coupling medium behind the surface).

The medium is saved with the results (medium.mat) and can be reused for later frames (reuseMedium).

Why the medium is random but reproducible

The draw uses MATLAB's global random stream. PROTEUS no longer needs the Statistics toolbox for it: random_truncated_normal reproduces random(truncate(makedist('Normal'),-c,c)) bit for bit (inverse CDF with erfc). The tests check this against stored toolbox draws.

n = −√2 · erfcinv( 2·[Φ(−c) + u·(Φ(c) − Φ(−c))] ),  u ~ U(0,1)
Setting up · the transducer

A transducer is a cloud of integration points

get_element_geometry defines where the elements are (flat array at x = 0, or a kerf-free arc for curved arrays). get_transducer_integration_points covers each element with points spaced dy/IntegrationDensity laterally (along the arc for curved arrays) and dz/IntegrationDensity in elevation; each point carries the weight (area)/(dy·dz).

get_transducer_integration_delays adds the acoustic lens: each point is delayed by its path difference to the elevation focus, √(z² + F²)/c. The same points are used to emit (source) and to receive (sensor), so the element directivity and the lens come out of the simulation rather than a model.

Setting up · points on a grid

Off-grid points: a band-limited delta on the grid

How a point source or sensor lives on the k-Wave grid

The integration points rarely coincide with grid nodes. define_sensor_transducer → update_sensor_fast represents each point by a band-limited delta function: the product of 1-D sinc functions centred on the point, truncated to ±4 nodes (evaluate_delta_function; Wise et al., JASA 2019).

  • Sensor: k-Wave records p on the mask nodes; the pressure at the points is ppoints = W · pmask.
  • Source: the same matrix transposed spreads each point's signal over the nodes, smask = Wᵀ · spoints.

Bubbles use exactly the same mechanism (define_sensor_MB). With OnGrid = true each point simply snaps to its nearest node.

Setting up · points on a grid

k-Wave grid and integration points are two different things

k-Wave grid

Regular nodes filling the whole domain, where the wave is computed. Spacing is set by the wavelength. It knows nothing about the transducer.

Integration points

Continuous positions on the transducer surface, a description of the probe. They sit anywhere between nodes, and their number depends on the density you choose.

The link: mask and weights

The mask is every node within ±4 cells of some point, a band around the surface. W connects the two, so all points must lie inside the grid, in the same orientation. Otherwise: pointsOutsideGrid.

Setting up · the transmitted pulse

Voltage → transducer → pressure

get_voltage_signal makes the driving waveform (three-level, cosine with a rectangular or Hann envelope, or custom). get_pressure_signal convolves it with the transmit impulse response, p = conv(V, IR)/Fs, and scales it to the requested peak negative pressure (or keeps the voltage scale in 'Voltage' mode). The impulse response is a 3rd-order Butterworth band-pass between the −6 dB limits (estimate_impulse_response), or a measured one from Transducer-models/<Type>.mat (calibrated; C1-6D today).

Setting up · beam forming in transmit

Transmit delays shape the wavefront

compute_delays gives each element a firing time so that their wavelets add up to the wanted wavefront:

  • plane wave at angle θ: τ = y·sinθ/c
  • focused at F: elements far from the focus fire first, τ = (Dmax − |E − F|)/c
  • diverging from a virtual source behind the array: τ = (|E − F| − Dmin)/c
  • curved, infinite focus: a diverging wave from the centre of curvature (all delays 0 at θ = 0).

All delays are shifted so that the first element fires at t = 0. In the k-Wave source, each integration point gets its element delay plus its lens delay, applied as a phase shift in the frequency domain (define_source_transducer).

Interactive: every element emits a circular wavelet (blue) when its delay elapses. Where many wavelets coincide they add up in phase: that is the transmitted wavefront (red). Focus at ±80 mm behaves as a near plane wave; negative focus = virtual source behind the array; slide to 80 for "infinite".

The wave solver · k-Wave

How k-Wave propagates the wave

Linear(ised) acoustics as three first-order equations

∂u/∂t = −(1/ρ0) ∇p   (momentum)
∂ρ/∂t = −ρ0 ∇·u − u·∇ρ0 + Sm   (mass, with mass source)
p = c0² (ρ + d·∇ρ0 + nonlinear + absorption terms)

k-Wave solves them with a k-space pseudospectral method: spatial gradients are computed with FFTs (exact for band-limited fields, so 2–6 points per wavelength suffice instead of ~15 for finite differences), and a k-space correction sinc(c0kΔt/2) makes the time stepping exact for a homogeneous medium.

run_simulation calls kspaceFirstOrder3D (MATLAB), …3DC (C++ CPU, default) or …3DG (CUDA) with a PML outside the domain. Staggered grid: velocity lives half a cell from pressure, hence the dx/2 shifts and the "k-Wave correction" in the reconstruction.

PML = perfectly matched layer: an absorbing band around the domain that swallows outgoing waves, so the finite grid behaves like an infinite medium.

The heart of PROTEUS · main_RF

Three iterations couple the wave, the bubbles and the probe

step 1 / 3

Why split it like this?

The bubble response is nonlinear and needs the incident pressure first; their echoes are tiny. So the transmit wave is simulated once per pulse (expensive, full-wave), and the bubbles are added on top as sources. In the default hybrid mode the echoes travel back with an analytical Green's function in a homogeneous medium (cheap); in full mode they are injected into k-Wave as mass sources (exact, slow).

Module 3 · microbubble-simulator

A bubble is a nonlinear oscillator driven by the wave

Rayleigh-Plesset with a shell

ρ(R R̈ + 3/2 Ṙ²) = pg(R) − 2σ(R)/R − 4μṘ/R − 4κsṘ/R² − P0 − pinc(t)
  • pg: polytropic gas pressure, with thermal damping (Prosperetti model)
  • σ(R): shell surface tension (Marmottant buckling/rupture, or the Segers tables)
  • κs: shell viscosity; μ: liquid viscosity; plus radiation damping

calcBubbleResponse solves it for a batch of bubbles at once (ode45 on simple_RP, nondimensional x = R/R0 − 1), from the pressure each bubble sensed. compute_bubble_mass_source resamples to 250 MHz, runs the batches (optionally with parfor), and returns the mass source q = 4πρR²Ṙ filtered and resampled back to the k-Wave time step.

What bubble–bubble interaction changes

Each bubble also feels the echoes of its neighbours, which shifts its response. A 2 µm bubble driven at 200 kPa radiates a peak of about 31 kPa at 20 µm, 6 kPa at 100 µm and 0.6 kPa at 1 mm (computed with PROTEUS). So it matters at high concentration and is negligible for sparse bubbles. NumberOfInteractions sets the iterations (default 0); each one repeats the ODE and the propagation for all bubbles. Microbubbles · what comes out

Size matters: resonance and mass sources

Why a mass source: a pulsating sphere injects volume dV/dt = 4πR²Ṙ. In the acoustic equations that is a mass source ρ·dV/dt, a monopole whose far field is p(r,t) = q̇(t − r/c)/(4πr).
Echoes back to the probe (hybrid mode)

Green's function propagation with attenuation

From each bubble to each point of the transducer

run_simulation_homogeneous averages the medium (c, ρ, α, y) and, for every bubble–sensor pair, applies the analytical Green's function of a power-law absorbing medium in the frequency domain (calc_scatter_attenuated; Kelly et al. 2008, Szabo).

  • Sensors = the transducer mask voxels; distances are snapped to a grid of step c·dt per source (gridded), which makes thousands of sensors cheap.
  • Memory is bounded by processing sensor chunks and source batches (MaxArrayElements).
  • A bubble never senses itself (zero distance → zero pressure).

This neglects the tissue heterogeneity on the way back (it is a small effect on weak, spherical echoes); the full mode keeps it by running k-Wave again with the mass sources.

Receive · compute_RF

From pressure on the grid to element signals

Acquisition schemes

Pulsing schemes isolate the nonlinear bubble signal

Standard

One pulse per frame. RF = tissue (linear) + bubbles.

sequence = {pulse}

Pulse inversion

Send +p, then −p. Summed, the linear (tissue) echoes cancel; the bubbles' even harmonics remain.

sequence = {plus, minus}; define_source_transducer negates the apodization

Amplitude modulation

Odd elements, even elements, then all: odd + even − all cancels linear echoes; nonlinear (amplitude-dependent) bubble echoes remain. Also combined with inversion.

sequence = {odd, even, all} or {odd, even, minus}

Each pulse of the sequence gets its own k-Wave transmit run (step ①) and its own bubble positions (bubbles move between pulses: TimeBetweenPulses). load_RF_data combines them when reconstructing.
Module 4 · delay-and-sum

From RF data to an image

For each pixel, delay-and-sum predicts when its echo reaches each element: transmit path (das_transmit_distance, the same model as the transmit delays) + return path to the element. It picks those samples from the RF data of every element and sums them. Summed in phase only at the true position, the hyperbolas of the RF data collapse into points.

All delays become one sparse matrix (compute_das_matrix, compute_das_matrix_general): image = M · RF, for every frame at once. Corrections: time to the peak of the two-way pulse, lens delay, k-Wave staggered grid. Then envelope (Hilbert), log compression, TGC. write_video makes the movie.

Hands on

Your first hour with PROTEUS

Install and check

  1. Clone the code. Until v1.1 is released, these slides describe the revamp branch of theodore-michel/PROTEUS.
  2. In MATLAB, at the repository root: setup_proteus('InstallKWave', true) reports what is missing and downloads k-Wave 1.4.1.
  3. cd tests; runProteusTests(Tag="Fast") runs the quick checks. Without the tag, it also runs small simulations (under a minute or two).

Simulate

  1. Download the example flow data from Zenodo into geometry_data.
  2. run MainGUI: look through the panels, save the settings, then Simulate pressure field.
  3. Generate the ground truth and run the RF simulation from the GUI, or from example_scripts/example_simple.m.
  4. Reconstruct with example_scripts/example_reconstruction.m.

Good first explorations

  • Change the focus and angle in the Transmit panel; compare the pressure fields.
  • Switch P4-1 to C1-6D and look at the domain in the Geometry panel.
  • Plot Frame_XX.mat: bubble positions and RF{1}.
  • Try pulse inversion: which echoes disappear in the image?
Expect long runs: realistic 3-D simulations need a large grid and usually a GPU or the C++ solver. Start with few frames and few bubbles. The test helper make_tiny_settings shows settings that finish in seconds on a laptop.

Conventions to remember: x depth, y lateral, z height. Paths go in proteus_local_paths.m, not in the code.
